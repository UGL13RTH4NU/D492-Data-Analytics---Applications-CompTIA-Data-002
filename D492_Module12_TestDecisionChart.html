D492 Module 12 — Statistical Test Decision Reference

Quick-scan chart for picking the right test on Data+ questions. Read variable types first, then match to the row.

🏷️ Variable Type Legend (read this first)

Every test choice starts with classifying your variables. Memorize these labels — the pills are used throughout this chart.

PillTypeExamples
Continuous Numeric, can take any value in a range. Meaningful arithmetic (means, std dev). Test scores, height, days absent, revenue, BMI, study hours
Categorical Discrete labels or groups. No meaningful arithmetic on the values themselves. Pass/Fail, Yes/No, Region (N/S/E/W), Very/Somewhat/Not Prepared, Male/Female
Ordinal Categorical with an order. Often treated as categorical on Data+. Likert scales (1–5), education level, satisfaction tiers

🌳 Master Decision Tree

Walk this top-down. Stop at the first match.

Q: What is the question asking you to do?

├── Compare AVERAGES / MEANS?
│ ├── 1 sample vs a known value or benchmark → One-sample t-test
│ ├── 2 independent groups → Two-sample (independent) t-test
│ ├── 2 related samples (before/after, matched pairs) → Paired t-test
│ └── 3+ groups → ANOVA

├── Measure a RELATIONSHIP between two variables?
│ ├── Both Continuous, just want strength & direction → Pearson Correlation (r)
│ ├── Both Continuous, want a prediction line / equation → Simple Linear Regression
│ └── Both Categorical → Chi-Square Test of Independence

├── Check if observed data fits an EXPECTED distribution?
│ └── 1 Categorical variable → Chi-Square Goodness of Fit

├── Estimate a RANGE for the true population mean?
│ └── → Confidence Interval (not a test — an estimation method)

└── Locate a single value relative to the mean?
└── → Z-score (not a test — a position measure)
🎯 The override rule: When a question says "relationship between two variables," do NOT reflexively pick Correlation. Classify the variables first. Two categoricals → Chi-Square. Two continuous → Correlation/Regression. This is the #1 trap in Module 12 questions.

🧪 The t-test Family — Comparing Means

All three use the t-distribution but answer different questions. Choose by what you're comparing.

Test Use it when… Variables Example stem
One-Sample t-test Compare ONE sample's mean to a known/expected/benchmark value. Continuous sample mean vs a fixed number "Is the class average significantly different from 75?" / "Compare average score to an external benchmark."
Two-Sample (Independent) t-test Compare means of 2 independent groups (different people in each group). Continuous DV, Categorical IV (2 groups) "Compare test scores of Classroom A (new program) vs Classroom B (control)."
Paired t-test Compare means of related/matched samples — same subjects measured twice, or pairs. Continuous, two measurements per subject "Compare student scores BEFORE and AFTER a study program (same students)."
ANOVA Compare means across 3 or more groups. Continuous DV, Categorical IV (3+ groups) "Compare average scores across freshmen, sophomores, juniors, seniors."
Excel: All four are in Data → Data Analysis (Analysis ToolPak). t-Test has three sub-options: Two-Sample Assuming Equal Variances, Two-Sample Assuming Unequal Variances, and Paired Two Sample for Means. ANOVA also lives there as Anova: Single Factor.

🔗 Correlation & Regression — Continuous Relationships

Both deal with two continuous variables. Difference: correlation describes, regression predicts.

Method What it does Variables Key Output
Pearson Correlation Measures the strength & direction of a linear relationship. Two Continuous r (range: −1 to +1)
Simple Linear Regression Models the relationship as a line of best fit; lets you predict DV from IV. 1 Continuous IV → 1 Continuous DV Regression equation + R², p-values, Multiple R

The key metrics — don't confuse them

Metric What it tells you Range How to read it
r (Pearson coefficient) Strength + DIRECTION of linear relationship. −1 to +1 Sign: + same direction, − opposite
|r| < 0.3: weak
0.3 ≤ |r| < 0.7: moderate
|r| ≥ 0.7: strong
R² (R Square) Proportion of variance in DV explained by IV. No direction — always positive. 0 to 1 0.23 → "23% of variance explained"
Remaining = other factors
For simple linear regression: R² = r²
Multiple R The correlation coefficient as Excel reports it in regression output. Always shown positive. 0 to 1 Equals |r| for simple linear regression. Don't expect a sign here — check the slope coefficient to know direction.
Adjusted R² R² penalized for adding more predictors. Used in multiple regression. ≤ R² Use when comparing models with different numbers of predictors.
p-value (in regression) Significance of each coefficient — is the relationship statistically real? 0 to 1 < 0.05 → significant relationship; ≥ 0.05 → can't rule out chance
🔑 The most testable distinction: r tells you how related. R² tells you how much one explains the other. They're not the same number (except by formula relationship), and they answer different questions on the exam.

📊 Chi-Square Family — Categorical Data

Both use the chi-square (χ²) distribution. Choose by how many variables.

Test Question it answers Variables Example stem
Goodness of Fit Does the observed distribution of one variable match an expected distribution? 1 Categorical "Do regional cell phone sales match the expected regional distribution?"
Test of Independence Are two categorical variables related, or independent of each other? 2 Categorical "Is preparedness level (Very/Somewhat/Not) associated with exam outcome (Pass/Fail)?"
⚠️ The recurring Module 12 trap: When you see "relationship between two variables" with categorical data, your brain may default to Correlation — but correlation requires continuous variables. Categorical × Categorical "relationship" → Chi-Square Test of Independence, always.

📏 Supporting Concepts (not tests, but tested)

ConceptWhat it isWhen to useFormula / Notes
p-value Probability of observing this data if the null hypothesis were true. Interpret any test result for significance. < 0.05 → significant, reject H₀
≥ 0.05 → not significant, fail to reject H₀
NOT the probability the null is true.
Confidence Interval Range likely to contain the true population parameter. When you want a range, not a single estimate. CI = mean ± margin of error
Excel: CONFIDENCE(alpha, sd, n) returns the margin
Default level: 95%
Z-score Number of standard deviations a value sits from the mean. Locate one value; check empirical rule (68/95/99.7%). z = (x − μ) / σ
Not a test — a position measure.
Null Hypothesis (H₀) Negative statement: "no effect / no relationship." Default assumption you try to disprove. "Means are equal." / "Variables are independent."
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁) Positive statement: "there IS an effect / relationship." The claim you're testing. "Means differ." / "Variables are associated."

🔑 Trigger Keyword Index

When you see these phrases in a question stem, they usually point to the test on the right:

  • "compare to a benchmark / known value / population mean" → One-sample t-test
  • "compare two groups / classrooms / samples" → Two-sample t-test
  • "before and after / matched pairs / repeated measures" → Paired t-test
  • "compare across three or more groups" → ANOVA
  • "linear relationship between [continuous] and [continuous]" → Correlation
  • "quantify the strength of the relationship" → Correlation (r) or Regression
  • "predict / line of best fit / regression line / trend line" → Simple Linear Regression
  • "how much variance is explained" → R Square (regression output)
  • "association between [categorical] and [categorical]" → Chi-Square Test of Independence
  • "observed vs expected / one variable / matches a distribution" → Chi-Square Goodness of Fit
  • "how many standard deviations from the mean" → Z-score
  • "range that likely contains the true mean" → Confidence Interval
  • "likely due to chance / statistically significant" → P-value interpretation

⚠️ Common Traps to Watch For

Trap 1 — The "Relationship" reflex: "Relationship between two variables" does NOT automatically mean correlation. Check variable types BEFORE choosing. Categorical × Categorical = Chi-Square Independence, not correlation.
Trap 2 — P-value inversion: p < 0.05 means the result is UNLIKELY due to chance (so it's significant). Distractors often flip this: "p < 0.05 means the results happened due to chance" is wrong.
Trap 3 — Correlation ≠ Causation: Any option claiming a correlation result "proves," "causes," or "guarantees" an outcome is wrong. Correlation describes association only.
Trap 4 — Low R² ≠ No relationship: R² = 0.23 means attendance explains 23% of variance — that's still a real (moderate) relationship. Don't dismiss it as "no relationship at all."
Trap 5 — CONFIDENCE() function output: Excel's CONFIDENCE() returns the margin of error (half-width), not the full interval. Apply it as mean ± output. The mean must be at the midpoint of the reported interval.
Trap 6 — Null vs Alternative confusion: Null hypothesis = negative statement (no effect). Alternative = positive statement (there IS an effect). Converting a research question "to a positive statement" creates the alternative hypothesis.
Trap 7 — "Line of best fit" on categorical data: Distractors sometimes pair "Simple Linear Regression" with a description involving categorical variables and a line of best fit. You can't draw a meaningful regression line through Pass/Fail data — that's the giveaway.

🎯 The 30-Second Process for Test-Selection Questions

  1. Identify both variables. Continuous or categorical? (Write it down if needed.)
  2. Identify the question type. Comparing means? Measuring a relationship? Checking a distribution?
  3. Match to the decision tree at the top of this sheet. Do not skip step 1.
  4. Sanity-check with the trigger keywords. Did the stem use words like "predict," "before/after," "association," "benchmark"?
  5. Eliminate the trap distractors. Anything claiming "proves," "guarantees," "causes," or that pairs a test name with an impossible purpose (line of best fit on categorical data, etc.).
