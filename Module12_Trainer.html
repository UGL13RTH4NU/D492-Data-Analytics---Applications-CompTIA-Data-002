Quick Reference — Distinguishing Characteristics

📊 Statistical Tests
T-Tests (parametric · continuous data · normally distributed)
All t-tests compare means. The subtype depends on what you're comparing to what:
One-sample — one group's mean vs a single fixed number.
"mean vs target""vs last year's average""vs spec"
Two-sample (independent) — two DIFFERENT groups compared.
"drug vs placebo""Group A vs Group B""different subjects"
Paired — SAME subjects measured twice (or matched pairs).
"before/after""pretest/posttest""same people"
Chi-Square (non-parametric · categorical data)
Compares expected vs actual counts in categorical data. No normality assumption.
Test of Independence — 2 categorical variables, are they related?
"preparedness × pass/fail""party × support""cross-tab"
Goodness of Fit — 1 categorical variable vs an expected distribution.
"15% fail, 85% pass expected""vs historical split""is the die fair?"
ANOVA (parametric · Analysis of Variance)
Compares means across 3 or more groups. When you'd want a t-test but have more than 2 groups, reach for ANOVA.
"three or more groups""5 departments""4 dosage levels""any of the groups differ?"
Simple Linear Regression (parametric)
One independent (predictor) and one dependent variable. Produces a best-fit line. Predicts / estimates a value.
"predict""one predictor""best-fit line""scatter plot trend line"
Correlation (Pearson's r · parametric)
Measures the strength of relationship between two equal variables. r is between −1 and 1. Does NOT prove causation.
"strength of relationship""r value""one changes, the other changes""not causation"
The main fork:
• T-Test: continuous means, 2 groups (or 1 vs a number)
• ANOVA: continuous means, 3+ groups
• Chi-Square: categorical variables (counts, not measurements)
• Regression: predict a value from a predictor
• Correlation: just measure the link, no prediction, no causation
Non-parametric alternatives (M11 mention): Random forest and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) are named in Module 11 as examples of techniques suitable for data that isn't normally distributed. They're ML methods, not classical statistical tests, and Module 12 doesn't drill them — but you may see them referenced elsewhere.
🎯 Sampling Methods (7)
Simple Random
Each member has an equal chance; selections are independent.
Stratified
Population divided into homogeneous subgroups; random samples from each. Used for imbalanced datasets to ensure all groups represented.
Systematic
Every nth element after a random start. Less bias than simple random; still easy.
Cluster
Population divided into clusters; randomly pick clusters; include ALL individuals in chosen clusters.
Multi-stage
Combines multiple sampling methods in stages.
Convenience
Whoever is easiest/closest. Quick but may not represent the population.
Snowball
Start with known members; they identify/refer additional members. Used for hard-to-reach populations.
⚠️ Hypothesis & Errors
Null hypothesis (H₀)
No relationship exists between variables.
Alternative hypothesis (H₁)
A relationship does exist between variables.
Type I error — False POSITIVE
Reject a TRUE null. You say there's an effect when there isn't.
Example: a healthy person is told they have cancer.
Type II error — False NEGATIVE
Accept a FALSE null. You miss a real effect.
Example: a sick person is told they're healthy.
Memory hook: Type 1 = 1 thing wrong (false alarm). Type 2 = missed it (silent failure).
📈 P-values, Alpha, Confidence Intervals
p-value
Probability the result happened by chance. p < 0.05 = statistically significant. Low p = repeatable. High p = likely chance.
Alpha (α)
The significance threshold. α = 0.05 corresponds to 95% confidence. α = 0.01 → 99%, α = 0.10 → 90%.
Confidence Interval
Range around the mean where the true population value is expected to fall. Bounds = mean ± CONFIDENCE result.
r value (Pearson's)
−1 to 1. ~1 strong, 0.4–0.7 moderate, ~0 none. Negative = inverse.
R² (coefficient of determination)
Proportion of variability in the dependent variable explained by the independent. Multiply by 100% to read.
🧮 Formulas Reference
Descriptive statistics (Module 11)
Mean (Average)
x̄ = Σx / n
Sum of all values divided by count. Excel: =AVERAGE(). Skewed by outliers.
Range
range = max − min
Highest value minus lowest. Simplest measure of dispersion.
Variance (sample)
s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)
Average squared distance from the mean. Uses (n−1) for sample data (Bessel's correction).
Standard Deviation
σ = √variance
Square root of variance — brings units back to original scale. Excel: =STDEV().
Z-score
z = (x − μ) / σ
How many standard deviations a value is from the mean. z beyond ±3 = outlier candidate. Excel: =STANDARDIZE().
Percentage Change
((New − Old) / |Old|) × 100
Positive = growth, negative = decline. Uses absolute value in denominator to handle negatives correctly.
Percentage Difference
|A − B| / ((A + B) / 2) × 100
Non-directional — neither value is a "baseline." Textbook example: 11 vs 9 students → |11−9| / 10 × 100 = 20%. Use for comparing two data points/sets side-by-side; use Percentage Change instead when there's a clear before/after or old/new.
Inferential statistics (Module 12)
Confidence Interval Bounds
CI = mean ± CONFIDENCE result
Excel's =CONFIDENCE(alpha, std_dev, size) returns a margin. Add/subtract from mean for upper/lower bound.
Alpha ↔ Confidence Level
α = 1 − confidence level
95% CI → α = 0.05; 99% CI → α = 0.01; 90% CI → α = 0.10.
Chi-Square Statistic (χ²)
χ² = Σ [(O − E)² / E]
For every cell: (Observed − Expected) squared, divided by Expected. Sum across all cells. Larger χ² = bigger deviation from expectation.
One-Sample t-statistic
t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)
Sample mean minus hypothesized value, divided by the standard error of the mean. μ₀ is the number you're comparing against.
Two-Sample t-statistic (simplified)
t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)
Difference between group means, scaled by the combined standard error. Larger |t| = more evidence the means differ.
Paired t-statistic
t = d̄ / (s_d / √n)
d̄ is the mean of the paired differences (after − before). Because each subject is its own control, variability is usually smaller than a two-sample t-test on the same data.
Pearson's Correlation Coefficient (r)
r = Σ((x − x̄)(y − ȳ)) / √(Σ(x − x̄)² · Σ(y − ȳ)²)
Value between −1 and 1. Measures strength AND direction of a linear relationship. Sign matches the slope of the trend line.
Coefficient of Determination (R²)
R² = r × r
Multiply by 100% to read as "percent of variability in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable."
Simple Linear Regression Line
ŷ = b₀ + b₁x
b₀ = intercept (where line crosses the y-axis), b₁ = slope (how much ŷ changes per one-unit increase in x). Fit by least squares.
Exam reality: The DA0-002 rarely asks you to calculate these by hand. It asks you to recognize what a formula returns, interpret a result (a p-value, an r, an R²), or pick the right function name (AVERAGE, STDEV, STANDARDIZE, CONFIDENCE). Memorize the shape and the meaning, not the arithmetic.
